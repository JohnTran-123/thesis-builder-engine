# Best Practices for Writing [institution] "Applying X to Enhance Y at Z" Theses

> **Source:** Lessons drawn from the [project] thesis realignment ([engagement end]). Saved as permanent reference for future thesis work.

---

## A. Before drafting

### 1. Treat the title as a binding contract
Every word in the title commits the thesis. Underline the verbs in red. **APPLY** ≠ EVALUATE ≠ ANALYZE ≠ DESIGN. Each verb produces a different thesis shape, a different table of contents, and a different set of research questions. The verb you pick determines what the rest of the thesis must do.

### 2. Pick the [institution] template before outlining
Three common shapes at [institution]:
- **3-chapter applied ([reference template] / [example firm]):** "Apply / Adopt / Implement X to Y at Z" — Ch1 overview, Ch2 firm + current state, Ch3 recommendations.
- **5-chapter empirical ([example firm]):** for hypothesis-testing studies — intro / lit review / methods / results / discussion.
- **3-chapter analytical:** for pure theoretical contributions, less common at undergraduate level.

Wrong template = wrong shape forever. The [reference template] template is the default for "Applying X to Enhance Y at Z" theses.

### 3. Build the TOC around the title verb, then read it alone
Once the TOC exists, read it independently of the title. If a stranger reading only the TOC cannot answer "what is this thesis arguing?" in the words of the title, the TOC is wrong. Fix it before drafting any chapter.

### 4. Map RQs one-per-chapter, all using the title verb
- RQ1 = Ch1 (theory of how X applies to Y)
- RQ2 = Ch2 (current state of X application at Z)
- RQ3 = Ch3 (how X should be applied at Z)

If RQ1 says "evaluate" but the title says "apply," the thesis is already off-rails before the first paragraph.

## B. While drafting

### 5. Direct claim, not scaffold
Every section opens with a substantive claim, not with "This section establishes / introduces / presents..." If the reader can skip the opening sentence and lose nothing, the opening is scaffold.

### 6. Voice = formal third-person with author markers
[institution] register is formal third-person. "The author observed that..." is the right marker for personal observation. Avoid "I" — but specifics still matter. [reference template] uses "the author" sparingly; when it appears, it is paired with a specific moment, date, or named role.

### 7. Specifics beat abstractions, every time
- ❌ "AI tools improved productivity"
- ✅ "On the Q1 document review prepared in late [engagement mid], Claude Code reduced first-draft time from three afternoons to a half-day"

The specific version is uniquely yours. The abstract version is AI-flagged.

### 8. Cap enumerations at 3-4 items
Humans do not naturally write "first / second / third / fourth / fifth / sixth / seventh." If you have eight opportunities, you actually have three to four themes. Group them. Eight items in a list is a structural mistake.

### 9. Disclose practitioner frameworks ONCE, with full caveat
If you use a firm's internal framework (such as a honest-scope disclosure model), disclose it once in the theory chapter with all the caveats — practitioner-developed, not derived from any single academic source, used descriptively only. Do not repeat the disclosure in every chapter.

### 10. Tables: introduce → analyze → bridge
- Before the table: "Table 1.1 summarizes the seven families."
- The table.
- After the table: "The table shows that X, which means Y for Z."
- Then bridge: "The classification is the basis for Section 1.3, which..."

Do not say "two observations should be drawn from the table." State the observations directly.

## C. While reviewing

### 11. Apply the three criteria
- **Intrinsic quality:** is each paragraph good content on its own merits?
- **Connectivity:** does each paragraph link to the one before and the one after?
- **Unification:** does each paragraph serve the thesis-level argument?

A paragraph can have great content and still be wrong if it does not connect or does not unify.

### 12. Read TOC alone — does it still match the title?
Mid-thesis, re-read the TOC by itself. Each chapter title, each section heading. Does the path from title to TOC to conclusions still hold? If you cannot trace it cleanly, something has drifted.

### 13. Read first sentences of every paragraph
This is the single most useful clarity check. Read only the first sentence of every paragraph in a section. Do they tell a story? If they read like "Author A says X. Author B says Y. Author C says Z," the section is a literature march, not an argument.

### 14. Author voice density: 1 specific sentence per 300 words
Every 300 words should have at least one sentence only the author could have written — a specific date, a specific role, a specific reaction, a specific number. This is the human signal that AI cannot fake.

### 15. Cross-section consistency
Use the same taxonomy in Ch1, Ch2, Ch3. If Ch1 defines five activity categories, Ch2 must use the same five, Ch3 must recommend per the same five. Mismatched taxonomies produce reader confusion and weaken the thesis.

## D. Anti-AI detection (if AI tools were used to draft)

### 16. [institution] voice IS the anti-AI strategy
Formal, ESL-characteristic, definition-heavy. Do not polish to native English. The ESL-academic register that feels "non-native" is the human signal. Polished prose is what AI produces by default.

### 17. Do not let AI rewrite AI
Once a paragraph has been AI-generated, only a human can lower its detection score. AI rewriting AI text raises detection from approximately 77% to 100% in tested cases. The fix path is: AI drafts → human rewrites with their voice → done. Do not loop the AI back over its own output.

### 18. Imperfections are signal, not weakness
The author's typos, slight grammar variations, ESL patterns ("the firm always do well" / "everything is carefully aligned first") — these are the human signal. Editors who clean these up reduce the human signal and raise detection risk.

### 19. Avoid the four AI tells
- Mechanical paragraph openings ("This section establishes...")
- Ordinal lists (first / second / third / fourth / fifth / sixth)
- End-of-paragraph filler ("Therefore, the implication is...")
- Three-item parallel structures ("This is X, which is Y, which is Z")

### 20. Specific moments are the strongest signal
Date + person + room + reaction is unfakeable. "During a [engagement period] strategic session, with the Chief Investment Officer focused on the capital raise, the team skipped the diagnostic stage." That sentence cannot be AI-generated.

## E. Process discipline

### 21. Title-content alignment check at every milestone
After outline. After each chapter draft. After assembly. After every major edit. Just re-read the title, then re-read the chapter intros. Do they match?

### 22. Do not let frameworks become the subject
A framework (such as TOE, RBV, or a practitioner pillar model) is an analytical tool, not the thesis subject. The thesis subject is the title's noun. Frameworks should serve the analysis, not be analyzed themselves.

### 23. Citation laundering is fatal
Never present a practitioner framework as if it were derived academic theory. If a firm built the framework internally, say so. The disclosure protects academic integrity.

### 24. Pilot evidence beats theoretical recommendations
A small-N pilot (one memo, one workflow, one before/after) is worth more than ten polished hypothetical recommendations. Even N=1 evidence with verification is stronger than a 25-page conceptual proposal with no application.

### 25. Voice density across the whole thesis
The thesis as a whole should have author voice density of about one specific-author sentence per 300 words. Not generic "the author observed that X" statements — specific moments. This is the binding constraint for AI-detection avoidance.

---

## The single highest-leverage habit

If only one habit is taken from this list: **at every checkpoint, ask "does the TOC match the title?"** Outline stage. Draft stage. Assembly stage. Re-read TOC and title side-by-side every time.

Many thesis realignments could be avoided if this check were performed routinely. Theses drift from their title verb gradually, and the drift is invisible to the author who sees only the current draft. The TOC-versus-title check catches drift early, when the cost of correction is small.
